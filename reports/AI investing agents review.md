# Claude can research stocks, but can't beat fees

Neither of the two AI investing products you asked about gives a retail investor a proven edge, and the public evidence says a Claude-driven trading agent run on AUD $500 will almost certainly do worse than one low-fee index ETF. **Barebone AI** is a real but young mobile research app from Barebone Group Holdings. Its "AI investment agent" mostly packages US market data and a large language model (LLM, the kind of AI behind Claude and ChatGPT) into plain-English answers. Its only performance claim, a "77% return" for the company's own internal fund, is unaudited. **"The Claude Portfolio"** (@theclaudeportfolio) has nothing to do with Anthropic. A University of Florida finance professor's group runs it: a multi-agent Claude Code pipeline that picks 15 US large-cap stocks every day under rules humans wrote. Its self-reported gain of **+19% against +12% for the S&P 500 over five months** is used to sell copy-trading subscriptions on the Autopilot app. Its prompts and code have not been published. In open, live trading contests Claude has **never won and has usually lost money**: about -30% to -42% in Alpha Arena Season 1 and about -32% to -35% in Season 1.5. Academic benchmarks find that most LLM agents fail to beat simply buying and holding. For an Australian with $500, building an agent is technically possible and, for your own account, legal. But per-trade minimum fees (A$6 per ASX order at Interactive Brokers), currency-conversion spreads and tax record-keeping eat any plausible edge. The sensible approach is to treat it as a learning project. Put most of the money in an index ETF, run the agent on a paper (simulated) account first, and only then let Claude *suggest* a small number of trades that you approve and place yourself.

## Barebone AI packages research neatly, but its edge is unproven

Barebone, sold as "Barebone - AI Stock Research" or "AI Finance Research", is a phone-only app for iOS and Android. It is published by **Barebone Group Holdings Limited** and reported to be based in San Francisco and founded in 2025 ([Caplight](https://www.caplight.com/company/barebone); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id6737490098)). The company says its founders are a former Goldman Sachs investment banker and a former engineer from Hanson Robotics, the company behind Sophia the robot. It has not publicly named them, and it has not disclosed any funding or investors ([Barebone review page](https://barebone.ai/resources/barebone-ai-review); [Caplight](https://www.caplight.com/company/barebone)). You ask questions in plain English and get back a summary that combines fundamentals (a company's financial results), technical analysis (price-chart patterns), news sentiment, corporate insider trades, US Congress members' trades and hedge-fund 13F filings (the quarterly holdings reports large US funds must lodge). The company says it has "20+ specialized research skills" and answers in "under a minute" ([agent-finder.co](https://agent-finder.co/reviews/barebone-ai); [barebone.ai](https://barebone.ai/resources/barebone-ai-review)). Its main pitch, according to a case study from its web-search supplier Linkup, is spotting **"emerging, viral narratives early on social media"** and turning them into research ([Linkup](https://www.linkup.so/case-studies/user-story-barebone-linkup)). Barebone does not say which AI model it runs on. It does not place trades. Portfolio connections are read-only, and it describes itself as "an informational tool" that gives no financial advice ([apppricinglab](https://apppricinglab.com/app/apple/6737490098)).

The app is free to download with a limited free tier. In the US store, **Plus costs about US$15/month and Pro about US$29/month or US$7.99/week**, with annual plans from roughly US$139 to US$349 ([App Store US](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id6737490098)). The weekly plan works out to over US$400 a year, which is a common subscription-app tactic. Reviews are few but positive: about **4.5 stars from around 82 to 102 iOS ratings** ([appshunter](https://appshunter.io/ios/app/barebone-ai-stock-research/id6737490098)), lower than the 4.8 the company quotes on its own site. Users praise the time saved when comparing several stocks, and one called it "half-baked" ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/barebone-ai-stock-research/id6737490098?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). The one independent reviewer gave it **7/10** and called it "a strong pick for retail investors who want faster research without becoming a spreadsheet analyst". The same reviewer flagged "unpublished pricing and thin public documentation" ([agent-finder.co](https://agent-finder.co/reviews/barebone-ai)). Many of the "best AI investing apps" rankings that put Barebone first are written by Barebone itself ([barebone.ai](https://barebone.ai/resources/best-ai-investing-apps)).

The warning signs are worth taking seriously. The headline claim, that Barebone's **internal fund "is up 77% over the last year"**, appears only in a supplier's marketing case study. It gives no fund name, size, audit or comparison with the market, and the same AI systems serve both the consumer app and the company's own fund, which is a potential conflict of interest ([Linkup](https://www.linkup.so/case-studies/user-story-barebone-linkup)). No registration with the SEC, FINRA or ASIC (the US and Australian regulators) was found. A 77% year during the 2025 AI-stock boom says little about skill. For an Australian the bigger problem is that its distinctive data (Congress trades, 13F filings, US insider filings) covers US stocks only. No source mentions support for ASX stocks, and an Australian App Store listing was not confirmed. **Verdict:** try the free tier on two or three stocks and compare the results with a direct Claude or ChatGPT question and a free tool such as Yahoo Finance or Simply Wall St. Avoid the weekly plan, and don't pay roughly A$45/month for Pro unless it clearly saves you hours on US stocks.

## The Claude Portfolio is a professor's rule-bound experiment sold through copy-trading

The account's own bio says it is **not affiliated with Anthropic** ([Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/theclaudeportfolio/)). It is run by **AI Finance Labs**, founded by University of Florida finance professor Alejandro Lopez-Lira, who researches LLMs and stock prediction. The same team runs seven AI portfolios, including ChatGPT, Grok and DeepSeek versions ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/retail-investors-put-27-million-into-claude-trading-portfolio-after-19-gain/); [UF Warrington](https://warrington.ufl.edu/directory/jose-alejandro-lopez-lira-y-ramirez/)). The Claude portfolio started on **3 March 2026 with a US$50,000 account** ([explainx.ai](https://explainx.ai/blog/claude-portfolio-autopilot-ai-trading-experiment-august-2026)). The trades are published on **Autopilot**, a US copy-trading app costing about **US$99/year**. Autopilot automatically mirrors a published portfolio into followers' own brokerage accounts ([MarketScreener](https://ae.marketscreener.com/news/can-chatgpt-and-grok-beat-the-market-ce7e5cdfd081fe24); [The College Investor](https://thecollegeinvestor.com/59796/autopilot-review/)). By August about **US$27M was reportedly copying it** ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/retail-investors-put-27-million-into-claude-trading-portfolio-after-19-gain/)). No source names the broker that holds the operator's own account or says whether its orders are placed by software or by hand.

According to a summary of the launch thread, the set-up is a **multi-agent pipeline**, meaning many separate Claude instances each given a narrow job ([X @CodeByPoonam](https://x.com/CodeByPoonam/status/2039048654368227408?lang=en)). It starts by screening every stock in the Russell 1000 (the 1,000 largest US companies) down to the top 50. Then 30 agents work in parallel, 15 arguing to buy and 15 arguing to sell. Another 15 agents model bull, base and bear scenarios with price targets. Finally, an "Agent of Agents" picks the final 15 positions. The written rules are to **beat the S&P 500 (via the SPY fund), keep no sector above 35%, and rebalance daily**, with results posted at 5pm. The agents draw on company financials, news and analyst data ([Linas newsletter](https://linas.substack.com/p/weeklyfintechpulse393)). The engine is reported as **Claude Code running Opus 4.6**. Claude Code is Anthropic's agent tool that can run on a schedule, read files and call other software. The agent gets "a single instruction" and works "under human-set rules" ([explainx.ai](https://explainx.ai/blog/claude-portfolio-autopilot-ai-trading-experiment-august-2026)). **No system prompt (the standing instructions given to the AI), code repository or data vendor has been published.** The marketing says "zero human approval needed", but Finance Magnates reports that Claude "is used for research and to generate investment ideas, while Lopez-Lira and his team set the strategy and risk parameters and control the entire investment process" ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/retail-investors-put-27-million-into-claude-trading-portfolio-after-19-gain/)). The most accurate description: Claude chooses the stocks and weights inside a box the humans built and supervise.

The reported performance, **+19.04% against +12.24% for the S&P 500** from March to early August 2026, is self-reported, unaudited and covers only five months ([X @theaiportfolios](https://x.com/theaiportfolios?lang=en)). Fifteen concentrated US large caps during an AI-led rally can beat the index simply by leaning towards technology, without any real stock-picking skill. Running seven AI portfolios also means one of them will usually be ahead and can be promoted. The sister DeepSeek portfolio reported larger gains still ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/ai-model-best-stock-trader-091501419.html)), and one Autopilot review found that headline returns "shrank considerably" once trade delays were counted ([CoolCuration](https://coolcuration.com/autopilot-app-review-uk)). Similar experiments warn against trusting early results. Nathan Smith's widely covered **US$100 ChatGPT micro-cap portfolio was up 23.8% after four weeks** ([Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/332826/high-school-students-chatgpt-trading-bot)), then **ended six months at US$82.88**, against US$111.68 for the same money in the S&P 500, after a **50% peak-to-trough fall** ([Substack Week 26](https://nathanbsmith729.substack.com/p/chatgpts-micro-cap-portfolio-week-e93)).

## Open-source trading agents all run the same five-step loop

The most popular open-source projects are research frameworks, not proven money-makers. **TradingAgents** (TauricResearch, roughly 70k-92k GitHub stars depending on the snapshot) sets up analyst agents, bull and bear researchers who debate, a trader and a risk team, all coordinated with a tool called LangGraph ([dev.co](https://dev.co/ai/frameworks/tradingagents); [oosmetrics](https://oosmetrics.com/repo/TauricResearch/TradingAgents)). **ai-hedge-fund** (about 50k stars) has agents role-play famous investors such as Buffett, Munger and Burry, and a portfolio-manager agent combines their views into a buy, sell or hold decision ([pyshine](https://pyshine.com/AI-Hedge-Fund-Multi-Agent-Investment-System/)). **FinRobot** writes equity research reports rather than trading ([arXiv 2411.08804](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.08804v1)). In practice, "Claude trading" usually means Claude Desktop or Claude Code connected to **Alpaca's official MCP server**. MCP (Model Context Protocol) is a standard plug that lets Claude use outside tools. Alpaca's server gives Claude 43 brokerage functions and a free simulated US$100K paper account, and carries the warning that it "can place real trades" ([Alpaca docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/docs/alpaca-mcp-server); [playbooks](https://playbooks.com/mcp/tedlikeskix-alpaca-trading)). The hobby Claude Code plus Alpaca bots on GitHub are almost all paper-only, and one candidly reports "a negative-expectancy result", meaning it loses money on average ([GitHub tatsuyawwp](https://github.com/tatsuyawwp/ai-trading-bot-experiment); [GitHub satyamdas03](https://github.com/satyamdas03/alpaca-trading-agent)).

Almost every project follows the same loop. It **fetches data** (prices, fundamentals, news). **Specialist agents analyse** it, often in a bull-versus-bear debate. A **decision agent outputs a structured instruction**, typically JSON (a machine-readable format) with the stock, buy, sell or hold, quantity, confidence and reasoning. Then **ordinary code that the AI cannot override** checks the instruction against hard limits before **sending the order to a broker**. Guides insist on starting with paper trading and a "hard risk layer the model can't override" ([MindStudio](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/build-ai-trading-agent-claude-code-alpaca); [mejba.me](https://www.mejba.me/blog/claude-ai-automate-stock-trading)). The table below shows how a typical project is laid out. It is pieced together from these projects' descriptions, not copied from one repository.

| Part of the project | What it does, in plain terms |
|---|---|
| `agents/` (one prompt per role) | The instructions for each "employee": analyst, bull, bear, risk manager, portfolio manager |
| `tools/` or `dataflows/` | Code that pulls prices, financials and news from free or paid data services |
| `graph.py` or main script | The running order: who speaks first and who has the final say |
| `config` and `.env` | Which AI model to use, which stocks are allowed, risk limits, and secret API keys (passwords for services) |
| Risk checks | Plain code that caps position size, blocks leverage and stops trading after a set loss |
| Broker adapter | The connection that actually places orders, usually Alpaca, paper mode first |
| Scheduler and journal | Runs the loop daily (cron, GitHub Actions or Claude Code routines) and logs every decision |

The common guardrails are a whitelist of allowed stocks, a maximum number of orders per day, a daily loss limit or "kill switch", a single switch between paper and live trading, and human approval before any live order.

## Claude has lost money in every major live trading contest so far

Live contests, where models trade real or simulated money on market data they could not have seen in training, are the best evidence available, and they are unkind to Claude. In **Alpha Arena Season 1** (Oct-Nov 2025, US$10k of real money per model, crypto perpetual futures, which are leveraged crypto bets), **Claude Sonnet 4.5 finished third of six but turned US$10,000 into about US$5,799**. Only the Chinese models Qwen3 Max (+22%) and DeepSeek (+5%) made money ([gncrypto](https://www.gncrypto.news/news/qwen-wins-alpha-arena-season-1-with-22-percent-returns/); [forklog](https://forklog.com/en/four-out-of-six-ai-models-suffer-losses-in-trading-tournament/)). Other coverage puts Claude's loss nearer -30.8% ([RootData](https://www.rootdata.com/news/412456)). Observers described Claude's style as a "100% long book… without hedging or stop-loss discipline", meaning it only bet on prices rising and never set automatic exits ([buildix](https://www.buildix.trade/blog/nof1-alpha-arena-lessons-ai-trading-byok-strategy-advisor-2026)). In **Season 1.5** (US stock tokens, Nov-Dec 2025), **Claude Sonnet 4.5 was down about 32-35%**, while a then-anonymous Grok 4.20 won ([bitcoinethereumnews](https://bitcoinethereumnews.com/tech/grok-4-20-leads-alpha-arena-season-1-5-with-22-38-return-on-trade-xyz/); [bitget](https://www.bitget.com/news/detail/12560605099629)). In HKU Business School's ten-model live currency-trading test from April 2026, **Claude "recorded more substantial losses"** while Kimi and Qwen led ([HKU press release](https://www.hku.hk/press/press-releases/detail/29241.html)).

The academic work agrees. **StockBench** found that "most agents did not beat a simple buy-and-hold baseline". The agents looked good in a rising market and poorly in the early-2025 downturn, and the study did not even charge trading costs ([arXiv 2510.02209](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02209v1)). **FINSABER**, which tested more than 20 years and more than 100 stocks, found that earlier LLM advantages "shrink considerably" and that the agents were too timid in bull markets and too aggressive in bear markets ([arXiv 2505.07078](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07078v5)). **Profit Mirage** found that risk-adjusted returns fall by roughly half or more once testing moves past the model's training cutoff. In other words, impressive backtests (replays of past data) partly reflect the model remembering what happened ([cited in HindsightBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18867)). One live benchmark, Agent Market Arena, found that agents "can trade profitably in real-time, often outperforming simple Buy & Hold", and that **the agent's design and risk style matter more than which AI model it uses** ([emergentmind](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2510.11695)). The fair conclusion is that contest winners change from season to season, the contests are too short and too noisy to rank models on skill, and no public, long-running, audited track record shows any model making money after costs. Claude's record so far is consistently below average.

## A A$500 Australian build is feasible, but fees outrun any likely edge

### What you would need, and the broker problem

A working agent needs four things: a Claude account or API key (paid, per-use access for software), somewhere to run it on a schedule, market data, and a broker that accepts orders from software. That last one is the bottleneck in Australia. **CommSec has no public trading API** (its third-party connection is read-only), and no API was found for **Stake or Pearler** ([SnapTrade](https://snaptrade.com/brokerage-integrations/commsec-api)). Alpaca, which almost every tutorial uses, was not confirmed to open live individual accounts for Australians. An older staff reply said it did not support individual accounts outside the US, though Australians can reach it indirectly through partners such as Pearler ([Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/are-non-us-entitled-to-trade-through-alpaca-api-now-or-not-yet/4520); [BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250423408320/en/Alpaca-Raises-52-Million-in-Series-C-Funding-to-Fuel-Global-Expansion-and-Category-Leadership)). The realistic options are listed below. Paper-trading availability for Australian accounts should be checked with each broker.

| Broker | API from Australia? | Cost on a small account |
|---|---|---|
| Interactive Brokers AU | Yes, long-standing API | ASX: 0.08%, **A$6 minimum per order**; US: **US$1 minimum** ([BrokerChooser](https://brokerchooser.com/fr/broker-reviews/interactive-brokers-review/interactive-brokers-australia); [IBKR](https://www.interactivebrokers.com.au/en/pricing/commissions-home.php?region=americas)) |
| moomoo AU | OpenAPI exists; AU terms not confirmed | US: **US$0.99 per order + 0.55% currency spread**; no inactivity fee ([moomoo AU](https://moomoo.com/au/pricing)) |
| Tiger Brokers | OpenAPI with an MCP server; AU eligibility not confirmed | Aimed at users "who have software development experience" ([Tiger docs](https://docs-en.itigerup.com/)) |
| Crypto (Independent Reserve, Swyftx, Kraken) | Yes | About **0.02%-0.6% per trade** ([Independent Reserve](https://www.independentreserve.com/blog/knowledge-base/swyftx-vs-independent-reserve)) |
| CommSec, Stake, Pearler | No trading API found | Usable only for placing trades by hand |

On A$500 these fees are severe. One ASX buy and sell at IBKR costs A$12, or **2.4% of the account**, so four round trips a month would cost about 10% of your capital every month. A US$100 round trip on moomoo costs about 4% of the position once brokerage and currency spread are counted. A crypto bot doing 20 round trips a month at 0.5% per side loses about 20% a month to fees alone.

### Claude costs: two conflicting price lists, one robust answer

The notes give **two conflicting sets of Claude API prices**, so check the current figures at [claude.com/pricing](https://claude.com/pricing) before relying on either. One source, an automated summary of Anthropic's pricing page, lists **Opus 5.5 at US$4/US$20, Sonnet 5.5 at US$2/US$10 and Haiku 5.5 at US$0.10/US$0.50** per million input/output tokens. A token is roughly three-quarters of a word: "input" is what you send Claude and "output" is what it writes back ([Claude pricing](https://claude.com/pricing)). Other sources list **Sonnet 4.6 at US$3/US$15, Opus at US$5/US$25 and Haiku 4.5 at US$1/US$5**, with about 50% off for non-urgent "batch" jobs ([finout](https://finout.io/blog/anthropic-api-pricing); [doit](https://www.doit.com/blog/anthropic-api-pricing)). The conclusion holds under either list. A **simple once-a-day agent** making one mid-tier call of about 20,000 words in and 2,000 out costs roughly **US$0.06-0.09 a day, or about US$1.30-2 (A$2-3) a month**. A **Claude Portfolio-style multi-agent debate** across ten stocks a day runs to roughly **US$100-240 a month**, which is 30-70% of a A$500 account every month. A **Claude Pro subscription** (US$17-20/month, about A$30) ([Claude pricing](https://claude.com/pricing)) works out to about 70% of the capital per year unless you already pay for it for other reasons. Scheduling is free: GitHub Actions, a service that runs code on a timetable, includes 2,000 free minutes a month for private projects ([toolradar](https://toolradar.com/tools/github-actions/calculator)). The rule of thumb: **at A$500, only the cheapest single-call design makes sense**, and elaborate multi-agent set-ups cost more than they could plausibly earn.

### Legal and tax position

No source found prohibits an individual from automating trades in their own account through a broker's official API. That is what those APIs are for, subject to the broker's terms and the ordinary rules against market manipulation. The legal line is crossed when you **run other people's money or sell your agent's signals**, which would likely count as financial product advice or dealing and require an Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL). This was not confirmed against primary ASIC guidance (see ASIC RG 36). ASIC repeatedly warns about fake "AI trading bot" products and unlicensed share-tip groups, so treat any ready-made bot sold to retail investors as high risk ([ASIC](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/news-items/scam-alert-scammers-luring-investors-onto-fake-crypto-asset-trading-platforms/)). One US obstacle has gone: FINRA's **US$25,000 "pattern day trader" minimum was scrapped from 4 June 2026**, although some brokers have until October 2027 to adopt the change ([Schwab](https://advisorservices.schwab.com/story/sec-approves-scrapping-25000-day-trader-minimum)).

Tax is a quieter cost. The ATO treats most people as **investors under capital gains tax (CGT)**. The **50% CGT discount applies only to assets held for at least 12 months**, so an agent that trades often gives that discount up, and every crypto-to-crypto swap is a separate taxable event ([Koinly](https://koinly.io/guides/crypto-tax-australia)). Australia has no fixed-day "wash sale" rule, but the ATO uses anti-avoidance law (TR 2008/1) against selling at a loss and quickly buying back to cut tax, and has warned of "swift compliance action" ([ATO](https://www.ato.gov.au/media-centre/wash-sales-the-ato-is-cleaning-up-dirty-laundry)). In practice the agent must record every trade in AUD (date, cost, proceeds, fees) from day one, and it must not be designed to sell and rebuy around 30 June.

### The benchmark you are trying to beat

The alternative is cheap and hard to beat. Index ETFs (funds that simply hold a whole market) such as **A200 and IVV charge 0.04% a year, and VGS and DHHF 0.18-0.19%**, which is cents per year on A$500 ([Pearler](https://pearler.com/explore/invest/au-shares/compare/DHHF-vs-VGS)). Professionals mostly fail to beat them: **74% of Australian equity funds trailed the S&P/ASX 200 in 2025, and 87% over 15 years** ([S&P SPIVA](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-australia-year-end-2025/)). In FY24, **68% of Australian retail CFD traders lost money**. CFDs are leveraged contracts on price moves ([Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/etoro-dominates-australias-cfd-market-where-trader-loyalty-rarely-survives-a-year/)). As an illustration rather than a forecast, a typical 7-9% year on A$500 in an ETF is A$35-45. An agent spending A$50 a month on fees and API costs would need returns above 100% a year just to break even. The honest expectation is that the money you spend buys learning, not profit.

## A practical path: ETF core, paper agent, human approval

If you want to try it, do it in stages. In **stage one, put most of the A$500, say A$400, into one diversified ETF** (such as DHHF, or A200 plus VGS) through a cheap broker in a single purchase, and leave it alone. In **stage two, use Claude as a research assistant, not a trader**. In the ordinary Claude app (free, or a plan you already pay for), use a saved project with standing instructions. For example: "Each week, review my watchlist of five ASX/US companies, summarise news and results, and propose at most one trade with reasons and a sell-if-wrong level." Log every suggestion in a spreadsheet. This needs no code and costs nothing extra, and Barebone's free tier can be compared against it. In **stage three, paper-trade for two to three months**: track Claude's suggestions against what the ETF did, or, with help from a technical friend, connect a once-a-day script to a paper account (Alpaca's free simulated account, or IBKR or moomoo paper if available to you). The script should make one Sonnet- or Haiku-class API call (around A$2-3 a month) and run free on GitHub Actions. Only if the paper record beats the ETF after estimated fees should you move to **stage four: a capped live sleeve of A$100 at most, where Claude suggests and you approve**. Place trades by hand on a broker with low minimums, allow **no more than two trades a month**, no leverage, options, CFDs or margin, and stop entirely if the sleeve falls 20%. Do not sell or share the signals for money, avoid copy-trading products that cite unaudited AI returns, and keep an AUD trade log for tax time.

## Conclusion

The common thread is that the AI is the cheap part and the trading is the expensive part. Claude is genuinely good at reading filings, summarising news and setting out a bull and bear case, which is what Barebone sells and what the Claude Portfolio's agents mostly do. No public evidence shows that turning those summaries into frequent trades makes money, and the most credible live tests show Claude losing more than most models. The Claude Portfolio's real innovation is distribution: an academic experiment packaged into a copy-trading funnel, where the operator's incentives favour publicising whichever of seven AI portfolios is ahead.

For a non-technical Australian with A$500, the most valuable result of this project is a cheap education in why systematic investing is hard: fees, record-keeping and the tendency of early wins to fade. Used as a research assistant beside an index ETF, Claude adds real value at almost no cost. Used as an autonomous trader on a small account, it mostly turns the account into brokerage fees and API charges. If a future live contest or audited track record over twelve months or more shows an AI agent beating the index after costs, revisit the question. As of November 2026 no such record exists.
